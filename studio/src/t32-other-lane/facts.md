# T32 facts (checked 2026-11-06)

| Claim on screen or in the voice | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Scientists tested this with two lanes on a computer | Confirmed. Redelmeier & Tibshirani, Nature 401:35 (1999), "Why cars in the next lane seem to go faster": a computer simulation of two lanes with the same traffic, differing only in starting spacing. | https://people.brandeis.edu/~moshep/Projects/DoTheyReallyMoveFaster/133.redelmeier.pdf (paper, fetched today) ; Science News summary https://www.sciencenews.org/?p=25793 |
| Both lanes had the same average speed | Confirmed (same traffic characteristics in both lanes). | Paper above |
| Other cars passed you for longer than you passed them | Confirmed. In a typical 10 minutes the index car spent 43 one-second epochs being overtaken vs 33 overtaking (130%); the total number of overtakes and passes was equal (about 46 each). The imbalance favoured being overtaken in 70% of simulations. | Paper above |
| The cars that you pass are gone quickly | Confirmed in spirit: passing epochs were fewer for the same number of passes, so each pass is over faster. The animation is an illustration; the bars use the true 43 : 33 ratio. | Paper above |
| Small line: in a video test, most drivers said the next lane was faster; it was a bit slower | Confirmed. 120 driving students watched a 4-minute rush-hour video; 70% judged the next lane faster although its overall speed was slightly slower. Not spoken (max two numbers). | Paper above |

Caveat kept (row): a computer test and a video test, not real driving; lanes are not always equal. Shown as the two small lines; "average" is spoken.

Row line "But cars passing you stayed in view for longer!" changed to "But other cars passed you for longer than you passed them!" (what the paper measured: time being overtaken, not time in view).
