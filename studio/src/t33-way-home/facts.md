# T33 · Why does the way home feel shorter? — facts (checked 2026-11-09)

| Claim in the Short | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|
| The trip back on the same road often feels shorter than the trip out ("return trip effect") | Confirmed | van de Ven, van Rijswijk & Roy (2011), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 18:827–832, "The return trip effect: Why the return trip often seems to take less time" — https://jayscholar.etown.edu/facpubharvest/443/ (row source; could not be opened this run) · Springer release via ScienceDaily, 25 Aug 2011 (read): https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/08/110825123106.htm |
| "Scientists tested this on bus and bike trips." | Confirmed | Three studies, 350 people in total: a bus trip, a bicycle trip, and a video of someone cycling (ScienceDaily release). Row: bus n=69, bike n=93, video n=139 (per-study sizes not in the release; not shown on screen). |
| "They think the trip out took longer than expected. So people expected a long trip back." | Confirmed as the authors' explanation (said as "they think") | ScienceDaily release: the outward trip is underestimated, so it feels long; travellers then expect the return to be long, and it is shorter than that. The effect was strongest for people who said the outward trip was disappointingly long, and disappeared when people were told in advance that the trip would seem long. |
| Familiarity is not the reason | Confirmed (not used in the script) | Effect also on a different return route of equal distance (ScienceDaily; Roy: "You do not need to recognize a route to experience the effect"). Kept out of the script (one idea). |
| Caveat: the cause is not settled | Kept ("They think…" + on-screen source line) | Ozawa, Fujii & Kouzaki (2015), *PLOS ONE* 10(6): the effect showed up only when people judged the trips afterwards, not while timing them (20 men, small) — https://core.ac.uk/download/39332035.pdf (read) |
