| An engineer's chocolate bar melted near a radar machine. | Confirmed as THE STORY, not as a proven event. Lemelson-MIT: "the chocolate bar in his pocket had started to melt". HISTORY.com: a candy bar, 1945, "while testing an active magnetron". SMECC biography: a peanut bar. Wikipedia calls it a legend: "There are no credible primary sources that verify this story", and traces it to a 1958 Reader's Digest article by Don Murray. So the hook is a question, the script says "The story says", and the screen says "No lab record proves it." | https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/percy-spencer · https://www.history.com/news/microwave-oven-invention · https://www.smecc.org/percy_spenser_biography.htm · https://wiki.krisyotam.com/content/wikipedia_en_all_nopic_2026-03/Percy_Spencer (Wikipedia copy; en.wikipedia.org could not be fetched in this run) |
| He held popcorn near the machine and it popped. | Confirmed as part of the same story. Lemelson-MIT: "He held the bag next to the magnetron"; National Inventors Hall of Fame: "the kernels pop"; SMECC: "popcorn exploded all over the lab". "No fire" is the plain meaning of a radar tube test. | https://www.invent.org/blog/inventors/microwave + above |
| Next, an egg in a kettle exploded in a co-worker's face. | Confirmed as part of the same story. Wikipedia: "exploded in the face of a co-worker". HISTORY.com: "a burst shell". Safety line on screen. | as above |
| Invisible waves like these now heat your food. | Confirmed. "like these", not "the same", because the frequencies are not identical. | https://historynet.com/?p=13720877 · https://lemelson.mit.edu/resources/percy-spencer |
