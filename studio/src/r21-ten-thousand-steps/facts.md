# R21 · Do you need ten thousand steps a day? — facts (verified 2026-11-14)

Verdict: **MYTH** — 10,000 steps a day is not a must: in the largest pooled study, the drop in risk of death
levelled off at about 6,000–8,000 steps a day for adults 60+ and 8,000–10,000 for adults under 60.
Education, not medical advice.

| Line | Claim | Verdict | Source (read on the day) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | It began as a Japanese advert. | TRUE | Paluch et al. 2022, Lancet Public Health 7(3):e219–e228, Introduction: the 10,000-step goal "originates from a marketing campaign in Japan" (full text, UCL Discovery PDF https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10170017/1/1-s2.0-S2468266721003029-main.pdf). Harvard Gazette (June 2019, I-Min Lee): the number "may trace back to 1965", when a Japanese company marketed a pedometer called manpo-kei, "10,000 steps meter". |
| 3 | Studies tracked forty-seven thousand adults. | TRUE | Paluch 2022 abstract: 15 studies (7 published, 8 unpublished), 47,471 adults, 3,013 deaths. |
| 4 | Over sixty, benefits flattened near seven thousand. | TRUE | Paluch 2022: risk of death fell with more steps until about 6,000–8,000 steps a day for adults 60 and older, then levelled off. Card on screen shows 6–8K. |
| 5 | Ten thousand a must? A myth! | TRUE | Same paper: "little evidence" for the 10,000 goal; quartile medians 3,553 / 5,801 / 7,842 / 10,901 steps had adjusted hazard ratios 1 / 0.60 / 0.55 / 0.47 — most of the drop came well below 10,000. Lee et al. 2019, JAMA Internal Medicine (16,741 women, average age 72): 4,400 steps a day linked to 41% lower mortality than 2,700; risk fell until about 7,500 and levelled off. |
| 6 | Under sixty, that point was eight to ten thousand. | TRUE | Paluch 2022: about 8,000–10,000 steps a day for adults under 60. |

Caveats said in plain words / in the description:
- These are observational studies of death from any cause; they show links, not cause and effect.
- More walking is still good; the point is that benefits start well below 10,000 and 10,000 is not a magic line.
- The 1965 manpo-kei story is "may trace back" (Harvard Gazette); the paper says a Japanese marketing campaign. The pedometer in the photo is a generic mechanical pedometer, not the Japanese one (tag says "JUST AN AD", no date or brand claimed).
