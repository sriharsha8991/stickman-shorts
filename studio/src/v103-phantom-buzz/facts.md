# V103 facts (verified 2026-10-28)

| Claim in the Short | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "Two in three hospital staff felt it too" | **Confirmed.** 115 of 169 responding medical staff (68%, 95% CI 61–75%) at Baystate Medical Center (western Massachusetts) reported phantom vibrations. One hospital, one survey (176 of 232 invited responded). | Rothberg et al. 2010, *BMJ* 341:c6914 — https://www.bmj.com/content/341/bmj.c6914.long · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21159761/ |
| "Your brain is waiting for a buzz. So a shirt rub becomes a call. A twitch becomes a text." | **Confirmed as the authors' hypothesis, not a proven mechanism.** The paper: the sensation "may result from a misinterpretation of incoming sensory signals by the cerebral cortex"; the brain expects a call and "misinterprets sensory input according to this preconceived hypothesis"; candidate sensations "pressure from clothing, muscle contractions". The cause "remains unexplored". | Rothberg et al. 2010 (discussion) |
| "That's the researchers' best guess." | **Kept as the caveat** (cause not known; Wikipedia summary: "The cause of phantom vibrations is not known"; signal-detection framing, Kruger & Djerf 2016, secondary). | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phantom_vibration_syndrome |
| "Off vibrate stopped it for most who tried." | **Confirmed.** Of those who tried each strategy: taking the device off vibrate worked for 75%, moving it 63%, a different device 50%. | Rothberg et al. 2010 (abstract) |
| Slate payoff "Not a fault: your brain prefers a false alarm to a missed call" | **Adjusted.** No primary source states the "prefers" cost-trade-off for this effect; replaced with the paper's own expectation hypothesis + "best guess" caveat. | — |
| Not harmful (context, not spoken) | Only 7% found it bothersome or very bothersome; authors call it a tactile hallucination, not a syndrome. Not health advice; no health-edu claim made. | Rothberg et al. 2010 |
