| Claim in the Short | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|
| You can see a clock seem to stop when you look at it. | Confirmed as a named illusion, the "stopped clock" illusion or chronostasis. The paper opens with people moving their eyes to a silently ticking clock and the second hand seeming to freeze. "Never stopped" on screen: only the feeling changes. | Yarrow, Haggard, Heal, Brown & Rothwell 2001, Nature 414:302-305, DOI 10.1038/35104551: https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/333/2/Illusory_perceptions_of_space_and_time_preserve_cross-saccadic_perceptual_continuity.pdf ; abstract page https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v414y2001i6861d10.1038_35104551.html |
| In a lab, under one second felt like one full second. On screen 0.88 s. | Confirmed. Experiment 1, 30 people: eyes jumped to a number counter, the digit 1 was judged equal to the later 1 s digits when it stayed on screen 880 ms (22 degree jump) or 811 ms (55 degree jump). Controls stayed close to 1 s. Read through a fetched summary of the full paper. Only the 880 ms value is shown (one idea, max two numbers). | Yarrow et al. 2001 (as above) |
| Your eyes jump. Your brain may then fill that moment. | Kept as "may". Yarrow et al. say the brain extends the seen target backwards in time to just before the eye jump. Melcher et al. 2020 replicate the effect with cued eye movements but argue the data fit a stretch just after the jump better, so the explanation is not settled. On screen: "One explanation. Scientists still debate why it happens." | Yarrow et al. 2001; Melcher, Kumar & Srinivasan 2020, Scientific Reports 10:16922: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-020-73830-6.pdf |
| Row caveat: lab counter test, not a wall clock. | Kept. On screen: "Lab test: 30 people, a number on a screen, not a wall clock (Yarrow, 2001)." | Row caveat; Yarrow et al. 2001 |

Our own picture, not data:
- The hook and "It stopped!" show the hand hanging for longer than the real effect (3 s and 1.4 s against about 0.1 s). On screen: "Our drawing makes the stop bigger. The real stretch is about a tenth of a second." (1 s minus 880 ms = 0.12 s.)
- The test on the clock: the second hand ticks exactly every 30 frames (1 s at 30 fps), nothing else moves, line boil off, ticks and music silent. It is a fair try, but the lab jumps were 22 and 55 degrees and a phone is much smaller, so the feeling may be weak or absent on a phone. The video never says the viewer felt it ("can feel"). No study of phone-sized jumps was checked.
- The gap on the board is drawn to scale at the start of the bar, following the Yarrow backdating account; that placement is one explanation, not a measurement. Mr. Trick filling it is a joke picture of that account.
- While the clock is off screen (the board), its seconds are reset so the end loops back to frame 0.
