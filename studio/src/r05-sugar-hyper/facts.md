# R05 · Does sugar make children hyper? — facts (checked 2026-11-11)

Education, not medical advice.

| Claim in the Short | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "Before you blame the sugar, are your kids hyper? Why?" | The belief on trial (hook) | Common belief; the 1995 meta-analysis opens with it ("parents and teachers" widely believe sugar causes hyperactivity). |
| "Scientists pooled twenty-three blind tests." | Confirmed | Wolraich, Wilson & White 1995, JAMA 274(20):1617-1621 (PubMed 7474248): 16 reports met the criteria, giving 23 within-subject studies; sugar vs an artificial-sweetener placebo with children, parents and staff all blinded. |
| "Children got sugar or a sweetener, and nobody knew which." | Confirmed | Same: inclusion required a placebo (artificial sweetener) and blinding of subjects, parents and research staff. |
| "Their behaviour showed no clear difference." | Confirmed | Same: across 14 measures (behaviour, attention, school tests and more) every 95% confidence interval included zero; authors: sugar "does not affect the behavior or cognitive performance of children". |
| "The sugar rush is a myth!" | Confirmed, with the caveat below | Same conclusion. |
| "Mothers who only thought there was sugar saw more hyper sons." | Confirmed | Hoover & Milich 1994, J Abnorm Child Psychol 22(4):501-515 (PubMed 7963081; abstract read at uknowledge.uky.edu/psychology_facpub/13): 35 mother-son pairs, boys 5-7 whose mothers called them sugar-sensitive; every boy got a placebo (aspartame); mothers told "sugar" rated their sons as significantly more hyperactive. |

Caveat for the description (from the 1995 authors): a small effect, or an effect in a small group of children, cannot be ruled out. Lots of sugar is still bad for teeth and health; this Short is only about behaviour.
