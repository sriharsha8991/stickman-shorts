# R32 · Does MSG give you headaches? — facts (verified 18 Nov 2026)

Verdict: **MYTH** — in blinded tests where MSG was eaten with food, it did not cause more headaches than a dummy, and
the few reactions seen in people who blame MSG did not repeat on retesting. Glutamate is also found naturally in foods
like tomatoes and cheese. Caveat: large doses (3 g or more) taken without food caused mild, short-lived symptoms in a few
sensitive people; a typical serving of a food with added MSG has under 0.5 g. Education, not medical advice.

| Line | Claim | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Hook: "Skipping MSG? Before you do, does it cause headaches?" | question | — |
| 2 | Blind tests with meals found no extra headaches | TRUE | Obayashi & Nagamura 2016, *J Headache Pain* 17:54 (systematic review of human studies): with food, five papers (six studies) found no significant difference in headache incidence vs placebo (one exception: the female group in one study). |
| 3 | Retests did not repeat the few reactions (card: 130 people who blamed MSG · reactions did not repeat) | TRUE | Geha et al. 2000, *J Nutr* 130(4S):1058S–1062S, multicenter double-blind placebo-controlled challenge in 130 people who reported MSG sensitivity: responses were low, inconsistent and not reproducible; none when MSG was given with food. |
| 4 | Tomatoes and cheese have it too (glutamate) | TRUE | US FDA, "Questions and Answers on Monosodium glutamate (MSG)": "MSG occurs naturally in many foods, such as tomatoes and cheeses." |
| 5 | MSG gives you headaches? Myth! | TRUE (as MYTH) | Above; FDA considers adding MSG to foods GRAS (generally recognized as safe); FASEB's 1990s review for FDA concluded MSG is safe. |
| 6 | Only big doses with no food bothered a few people (card: 3 g+ with no food · a serving has under 0.5 g) | TRUE | FDA Q&A: FASEB found mild, short-term symptoms in some sensitive people who "consume 3 grams or more of MSG without food"; "a typical serving of a food with added MSG contains less than 0.5 grams". Obayashi & Nagamura: without food, 4 of 7 studies found a difference, but many used strong MSG solutions that are easy to taste, so blinding was weak. |

Caveats in the description (plain words): the evidence is mixed for big doses on an empty stomach, and the review's
authors say better-blinded studies are still needed; if a food reliably gives you symptoms, talk to a doctor.
