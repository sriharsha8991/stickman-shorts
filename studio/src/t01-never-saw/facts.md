| Claim on screen or in the voice | Verdict | Source |

| A list of related words makes people remember a word that was never on it | Confirmed. Roediger & McDermott (1995), "Creating false memories: Remembering words not presented in lists", JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition 21(4):803–814. | https://personality-project.org/revelle/syllabi/classreadings/rm95/rm95.html (paper text, fetched today) |
| The 15 words on screen | Confirmed: the Exp. 2 "sleep" list as printed in the 1995 paper's method: bed, rest, awake, tired, dream, wake, night, blanket, doze, slumber, snore, pillow, peace, yawn, drowsy. (A later printing of the appendix lists snooze and nap in place of night and pillow; both versions are DRM sleep lists.) | Paper above |
| "In one test, people heard lists like this." | Confirmed. Exp. 2: 30 Rice University students heard 16 lists of 15 words from a tape (about 1.5 s per word), then wrote down what they remembered. Changed from the row's "recalled it" so the line says the words were heard. | Paper above |
| "About half the time, they still remembered sleep." | Confirmed as a rate across lists: "Subjects recalled the critical nonpresented word on 55% of the lists" (Exp. 2). Exp. 1 (12-word lists, 36 students): 40%. Small line on screen: 55% of lists. | Paper above |
| Viewer test on screen (words read, not heard) | Reading also produces false memories in later studies, usually a little less than hearing (Smith & Hunt 1998, "Presentation modality affects false memory", Psychonomic Bulletin & Review).
