# V105 · Why can't you tickle yourself? — facts (checked 2026-10-30)

| Claim | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Touch you make yourself feels less ticklish than the same touch from someone else | Confirmed | Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith 1998, Nature Neuroscience 1:635–640 "Central cancellation of self-produced tickle sensation" (fMRI, 6 people): "A self-produced tactile stimulus is perceived as less ticklish than the same stimulus generated externally." https://web.math.princeton.edu/~sswang/autism/blakemore_frith98_NN.pdf |
| The brain predicts the touch from your own movement and turns the feeling down | Confirmed (authors' explanation, supported by less activity in touch areas of the brain) | Blakemore et al. 1998: reduced tickle "due to a precise cancellation of the sensory feedback, based on specific sensory predictions"; cerebellum proposed as the predictor |
| People moved a robot with one hand that touched their other hand; a delay made it more tickly | Confirmed | Blakemore, Frith & Wolpert 1999, J Cognitive Neuroscience 11:551–559 (16 adults): tickliness "increased significantly with increasing delay" from 0 to 200 ms; at 200–300 ms it matched an outside touch; only 2 of 16 noticed the delay. https://Gwern.net/doc/psychology/neuroscience/1999-blakemore.pdf |
| "A fifth of a second" = 200 ms | Confirmed | Same (200 ms delay) |
| "It does not work very well" (not "never") | Caveat kept: self-touch is weaker / less ticklish, not zero | Both papers rate it "less" ticklish |
| Slate payoff "Your brain predicts your own touch and cancels it" | Kept, said in plain words ("makes the touch feel weaker"); "cancels" softened because the effect is partial | — |
| Popular write-up | Context | Scientific American, "Why can't a person tickle himself?" https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/why-cant-a-person-tickle/ |
