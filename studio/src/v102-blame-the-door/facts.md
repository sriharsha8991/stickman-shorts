# V102 · Why you forget why you walked into a room — fact check (2026-10-28)

| Claim (as used in the Short) | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Walking through a doorway is linked to more forgetting than walking the same distance inside one room ("Right after a door, they forgot more" / "Same walk, no door? Fewer mistakes.") | **Confirmed** (lab finding). Virtual environment of 55 rooms: participants carried an object, set it down, picked up another; memory probes after a doorway vs. halfway across a large room. More errors after doorways. Replicated in a real-world set of rooms. | Radvansky, Krawietz & Tamplin (2011), *QJEP* 64(8):1632–1645, doi:10.1080/17470218.2011.571267; summary: https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-walking-through-doorway-increases-forgetting |
| "Your brain saves life in scenes. A doorway ends the scene. The last room gets filed away." | **Confirmed as the authors' explanation** (event-model / event-boundary account: a doorway is an event boundary; the earlier event is stored and becomes less available). Simplified wording. | Radvansky et al. 2011 (Event Horizon model); BPS Research Digest summary above |
| "But a newer test found a catch. It only showed up when minds were busy." | **Confirmed.** Four experiments (VR, video, real walking). No doorway effect in VR without extra load (Bayes favoured null), in passive video, or in real walking; the effect appeared only in Experiment 2, where participants also counted backwards aloud (working-memory load). | McFadyen, Nolan, Pinocy, Buteri & Baumann (2021), *BMC Psychology* 9:41, doi:10.1186/s40359-021-00536-3, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7938580 |
| "You didn't lose your memory." | **Kept as reassurance**: the effect is a normal memory effect shown in healthy (student) participants. Not a medical claim. | Both papers above |
| "So carry one thought, not three." | **Framed as a tip, not a finding**: follows from the 2021 result (effect only under load). Nobody tested this exact advice. | McFadyen et al. 2021 |
| Slate payoff "go back through the door" | **Dropped.** In Radvansky 2011 (Exp. 3) going back into the original room did *not* restore memory (no better than the new room), and McFadyen 2021 saw no return effect either. Replaced with the busy-mind payoff. | Radvansky et al. 2011; McFadyen et al. 2021 |
| Slate twist "It's not age" | **Softened** to "You didn't lose your memory" (participants were students, but the study doesn't compare ages). | — |
