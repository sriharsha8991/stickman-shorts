# CH2 What it reads
Most people think it just shows what's popular.
Or that it only counts views.
But YouTube's own help page lists the signals.
And they're mostly about you.
Signal one: your watch history.
Two: what you search for.
Three: the channels you subscribe to.
Four and five: your likes and dislikes.
Six: every time you tap "Not interested."
Seven: "Don't recommend channel."
And eight is the sneaky one.
Sometimes, it asks you to rate a video.
Why ask, if it already knows what you watched?
Because watch time can't tell if you were happy.
You can watch something for an hour and hate it.
So the survey checks the machine's own guess.
It's not reading your mind.
It's reading what you do.

# CH3 The ranking race
Here's a simplified picture.
Think of a talent show with an endless line.
In a twenty sixteen paper, YouTube engineers described two steps.
Step one: from an enormous pile of videos,
grab a few hundred that might fit you.
Step two: give every one of those a score.
Only the top few make the final.
That's your screen.
So what was that score chasing?
Not clicks. Clicks reward clickbait,
videos people don't even finish.
The paper aimed for expected watch time instead.
And today, YouTube says surveys help measure satisfaction too.
So the race isn't "most popular wins."
It's "most likely to keep you watching."

# CH4 The loop
And here's the part nobody tells you.
That race runs again every time you come back,
using what you did last time.
Watch three cooking videos, you'll likely see more cooking.
Watch those, and the next guess leans harder.
You watch, it learns, it offers more of that.
The circle can get smaller and smaller.
That's why two people can open the same app
and see two completely different worlds.
It's not evil, and it doesn't trap everyone.
It's just doing its job: guessing well.
