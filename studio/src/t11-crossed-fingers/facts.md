# T11 facts (checked 2026-11-06)

| Claim on screen or in the voice | Verdict | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Crossed fingers on one small round thing can feel like two things | Confirmed. In a blindfold lab test, about 93% of answers were "two" with crossed fingers, about 16.7% with fingers side by side. Small group (15 patients with Parkinson's, 15 healthy people), so no number on screen, as the row's caveat asks. | Fiorio et al. 2014, PLoS ONE 9(2): e88686, read via https://core.ac.uk/download/217535047.pdf |
| A pea or your nose both work | Confirmed (secondary). Illusions Index: marble or frozen pea. Popular Science + Gustavus psychology blog: the nose works too. Row said "a small round thing"; the nose is added so every viewer has something to test with. | https://www.illusionsindex.org/i/aristotle-s-illusion ; https://www.popsci.com/story/science/head-trip-aristotle-illusion ; https://blog.gustavus.edu/2020/04/09/a-hands-down-simple-illusion-the-aristotle-illusion/ |
| Closed eyes make it stronger | Confirmed. Illusions Index says so. Small on-screen line during the test. | https://www.illusionsindex.org/i/aristotle-s-illusion |
| Normally, those two sides of your fingers touch two different things | Adjusted. Row said "never normally meet", which is not right: these sides do not meet at all. Hanover's page: these sides face away from each other and usually get touches from separate objects. | https://isle.hanover.edu/Ch01Intro/Ch01Aristotle.html ; Illusions Index above |
| So, your brain seems to decide there are two things | Adjusted. Row said "decides"; "seems to" added because the row's caveat says the explanation is not settled. Illusions Index presents it as a plausible account; Fiorio 2014 proposes a similar one. Small line on screen: "The reason is a best guess." | Above |
| Most people feel two, not everyone | Confirmed (Hanover and Gustavus: "most people"; Illusions Index: "should"). On-screen small line, so a viewer who feels one is not told they are wrong. | Above |

Not used: the 93% and 16.7% (small clinical sample, row caveat). Salt and pepper are our illustration of "two different things", not from a study. The ghost peas show what the brain reports; no second pea exists in the picture.
